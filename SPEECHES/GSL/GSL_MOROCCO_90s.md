## THE SPEECH (FINAL)

**[COLD OPEN — no greeting:]**

Most of the law of warfare rests on an assumption it never had to state: a human being decides to take a life. Autonomous weapons test that assumption directly.

Hold existing humanitarian law against that test and the gap becomes precise. The Secretary-General's review reached two conclusions: no international instrument specifically regulates these systems — and accountability cannot be transferred to a machine. The first reported autonomous engagement sits on the UN record, still unresolved.

Some delegations say existing law is sufficient. Then answer one question: when a machine chooses the target, who is responsible — **under which instrument, by which standard?** [PAUSE — 2 seconds] If the law sufficed, that answer would exist. It does not.

Honourable Chair, distinguished delegates — Morocco operates advanced unmanned systems under human command, lawfully, and we do not propose to ban this technology. We propose that the decision to take life remain under **meaningful human control** — binding, not assumed.

Three steps make it real: adopt the consensus characterization built in Geneva; require weapons reviews against shared criteria, repeated when software changes the system; and record engagements, so responsibility always attaches to identifiable human command — with capacity support for states that need it.

Those who call binding law premature have had a decade to answer this question. It stands unanswered. Machines may assist human judgment; they must never inherit human responsibility. **[PAUSE]**

## 🧩 STRATEGIC ANNEX

**D. The strongest argument against Morocco (steelmanned):** IHL (distinction, proportionality, precautions) governs *every* weapon; Article 36 and command responsibility already attach accountability to humans; a binding text is premature, threatens CCW's fragile consensus, and "meaningful human control" is too vague to be law — while the P5 producers won't join, so the norm is hollow from day one.

**E. How the speech pre-empts it:** (1) It never denies IHL applies — it attacks *specificity*, using the Secretary-General's own two conclusions, so the sufficiency camp must argue with the SG, not with us. (2) The triple question converts "sufficiency" from a slogan into a citation problem: name the instrument, name the standard, name who answers. (3) It disavows prohibition → no security-dilemma sting, no universality scare; nothing is asked of any arsenal except a human decision. (4) Reviews stay national (criteria shared, not custody) → sovereignty-safe; capacity support answers the Global South objection before it is raised. (5) Morocco's operator status is flipped from conflict to credibility: the standard is *operable* — we fly it daily.

**F. Single strongest line:** "When a machine chooses the target, who is responsible — under which instrument, by which standard?"

**G. Three alternative openings (smarter, not louder):**
1. "The Secretary-General has advised this Assembly that accountability cannot be transferred to a machine — and that no instrument specifically regulates these systems. Two facts. Our work begins from them."
2. "Geneva has produced a characterization, guiding principles, and more than a decade of discussion — but not one binding rule. Morocco's question is narrow: what, exactly, does existing law require of a machine that selects a target?"
3. "Every rule of warfare this committee enforces assumes a human decision at the moment force is used. Autonomous weapons are the first challenge to the assumption itself — and unwritten assumptions do not survive contact with technology."

**H. Three alternative closings:**
1. "If existing law already guarantees human responsibility, the floor is open — show us the provision. Until then, the gap is not a position; it is a fact."
2. "Control that cannot be verified is not control, and responsibility that cannot be identified is not responsibility. Morocco is ready to build both."
3. "Let every arsenal keep its technology — and let every decision keep its human. Morocco sees no conflict between the two, and neither should this committee."

**I. Five likely POIs + responses:**
1. **"Define meaningful human control — it has no agreed legal meaning."** → "Exactly why the instrument is needed. The parameters are known — predictability, reliability, and the human ability to intervene or abort — sensible national practice already uses them. Shared review criteria turn a principle into a standard. 'Undefined' is the case *for* the law, not against it."
2. **"Article 36 plus command responsibility is sufficient."** → "Sufficient for whom? Barely a dozen states run reviews, to no shared standard, with no reporting — and several major military powers are not even party to the instrument containing that duty. A duty without a standard is a hope, not a rule."
3. **"A binding push now undermines the CCW consensus track."** → "A decade produced a characterization and principles — we propose using both. Codifying what consensus built does not undermine Geneva; it arrives *prepared*."
4. **"Morocco endorsed the 2023 Political Declaration — voluntary then, binding now?"** → "Floors are built on, never hidden behind. The Declaration governs the responsible use of autonomy *by humans*. It is silent on the absence of a human decision. That silence is this agenda."
5. **"You operate armed drones — convenient morality."** → "Under human command, within humanitarian law — which makes us the proof that the standard is operable, not its exception. The proposal disciplines the *decision*, not the tool; any state flying under human control loses nothing by signing."
